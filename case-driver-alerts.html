The problem

A warning that makes a driver look away is a hazard of its own.

Our device beeped for everything, with the same sound for a phone in hand and a truck too close. Drivers couldn't tell what each beep meant without looking at the screen.

Many learned to ignore it, and some unplugged the device entirely. Fleets were paying for safety technology their drivers had switched off.

Research: what you did and what you learned. Use real quotes from users if you have them.

What we learned

Drivers trust alerts that respect their attention.

I spent six days on ride-alongs across long-haul and city routes, interviewed 15 drivers, and analysed which alerts were most often followed by the device being switched off.

Noise breeds distrustAfter a few false alarms, drivers stopped believing any alert.It beeps even when I'm driving fine.
Language mattersText-only alerts failed for drivers who read a different script.I don't read English while driving.
Glance, then actDrivers had under a second to understand before acting.Just tell me what to do.

Process

From one beep for everything to a language drivers learn once.

  1. Map every alertListed every warning by severity and how fast the driver had to respond.
  2. Design the languagePaired each severity with a distinct tone, light pattern and icon.
  3. Test in real cabsRan prototypes on real routes with drivers before writing production code.
  4. Roll out by fleetShipped to pilot fleets first and tracked unplug rates and risky events weekly.

Key decisions: 2 or 3 choices that shaped the outcome. For each, what you chose, what you rejected, and why.

Key decisions

1. One alert language

Tone, light and icon mean the same thing everywhere, so drivers learn it once. We rejected spoken voice alerts as the default because they were slower to understand and harder to localise.

2. Escalate, don't shout

Alerts start gentle and rise only if the risk does. This kept low-risk moments quiet, which is what rebuilt drivers' trust in the loud ones.

Impact: numbers first, then one quote from a customer or stakeholder.

Impact

Drivers kept it on, and roads got safer.

Across pilot fleets, high-risk events fell by 22% and devices unplugged by drivers dropped by 60%. The alert language became the standard for every new feature.

"Now when it beeps, I know it matters."

Reflection: what you'd do differently. Shows self-awareness, which hiring managers value in leaders.

Looking back

I'd test with night-shift drivers sooner. Fatigue changed how people perceived sound and light more than we expected, and our first night-time tests forced a late change to brightness levels.
