A fleet dashboard that ranks risk, not noise

Fleet managers were drowning in AI-detected events. I led the redesign that turned thousands of alerts into a short list of drivers to call today, and cut incident review time by 45%.

Context and problem. Who is the user, what was broken, why it mattered to the business. Three short paragraphs at most.

The problem

Every event was treated as urgent, so nothing was.

Our cameras flagged every risky moment on the road: drowsiness, phone use, harsh braking, close following. A mid-size fleet generated thousands of events a week, and the dashboard listed them in time order.

Fleet managers told us they had stopped opening it. The most dangerous drivers were buried under minor events, and some customers questioned renewing. The product was detecting risk well and communicating it badly.

Research: what you did and what you learned. Use real quotes from users if you have them.

What we learned

Managers don't want events. They want to know who to call.

I ran 12 interviews with fleet managers and safety officers, sat in on two morning briefings, and reviewed six months of usage data.

Time is the constraintManagers had 20 minutes a day for safety, at best.I need the five drivers to worry about, not five hundred clips.
Patterns beat momentsOne harsh brake is noise. Three drowsy nights in a row is a call.Tell me if it's getting worse.
Coaching, not blameGood managers wanted to help drivers improve, not punish them.If I just shout, they quit.

Process: the 4 to 6 steps you actually took. Show how you led, not just what you drew.

Process

From thousands of events to one ranked list.

  1. Reframe the goalAgreed with founders and sales that success meant drivers coached, not events shown.
  2. Define a risk scoreWorked with the ML team to combine event type, frequency and trend into one score per driver.
  3. Prototype with real dataTested three dashboard concepts with eight managers using their own fleet's data.
  4. Ship in slicesReleased ranking first, then review, then coaching, measuring each step.

Key decisions: 2 or 3 choices that shaped the outcome. For each, what you chose, what you rejected, and why.

Key decisions

1. Rank drivers, not events

We replaced the time-ordered event feed with a list of drivers sorted by risk score. We considered smarter filters instead, but filters still asked managers to do the work.

2. Review and coach in one view

The clip, its context and a suggested coaching conversation sit together, so a manager can review and act without switching tabs. The full event history stays one click away for audits.

Impact: numbers first, then one quote from a customer or stakeholder.

Impact

Managers came back, and drivers got safer.

Within three months of launch, incident review time fell by 45%, coached drivers tripled, and high-risk events across pilot fleets dropped by 18%. Two customers cited the new dashboard in their renewals.

"For the first time, I open this every morning. It tells me exactly who to talk to."

Fleet safety manager, logistics customer

Reflection: what you'd do differently. Shows self-awareness, which hiring managers value in leaders.

Looking back

I'd involve drivers earlier. We designed coaching for managers first, and only later learned how drivers felt about being scored. Their input reshaped the language we used, and would have saved us a release.
